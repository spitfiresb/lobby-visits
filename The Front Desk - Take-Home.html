Who gets in

#Requirement
1A sign-up and a sign-in page. People create their own account; nobody is pre-registered for them.

This is a prototype, not production. Store accounts however you like — a file, browser storage, SQLite. We are not grading your cryptography. We do want two honest sentences in your note: what's fake here, and what you'd do differently with real visitors' data behind it.

04

Two kinds of user

Getting someone through the door is authentication. Deciding what they see once inside is the harder half, and it's where most people lose points.

admin — 3 people

Sees everything: visitor names, email addresses, the company they came from, who hosted them.

viewer — 5 people

Sees how busy the building is — counts, dates, host teams, visit types. Never a visitor's name or email.

A viewer must not be able to reach a name by any route you leave open. Not by editing the URL, not through a filter, and not by opening the network tab. Hiding a column while the data is still in the response doesn't count — that's the most common way this gets faked, and it's the first thing we check.

There's a design decision hiding here: what does a viewer see where a name would be? A dash, "Visitor", an initial, a number? Make a choice that leaves the page useful, and mention it in your note.

05

The API

The pages don't read the CSV. They call a small HTTP API that you write, and that API is where the access rules actually live. It all runs on your machine — no AWS, no cloud account, no deployment. Load the two CSVs at startup into SQLite, a JSON file, or just a list in memory. Any language, any framework.

EndpointDoes
POST /auth/signupCreates an account, if that email is on the allowed list.
POST /auth/loginStarts a session — token or cookie, your call.
GET /api/meWho am I and what am I allowed to see.
GET /api/visitsThe visit rows, filterable by date range and visit type, capped or paged.
GET /api/visits/:idOne visit.
GET /api/statsThe counts behind the summary — by day of week and by hour.

Those paths are a suggestion, not a spec. Name them how you like; just document what you built.

Five rules for it

  • Nothing returns data without a valid session. An unauthenticated call gets a 401 — a JSON error, not a redirect to HTML.
  • The roles are enforced in the API, not the interface. A viewer's response must not contain a name or email anywhere in the payload. Not hidden, not nulled under another key — absent.
  • Filtering and counting happen server-side. Sending all 2,011 rows to the browser and filtering there is the thing this rule exists to rule out.
  • Bad input gets a 400 with a message a human can act on — an end date before the start date, a nonsense visit type. Never a 500, never an empty 200.
  • It works from curl. Two worked examples in your README: logging in, and a filtered visits call.

How we'll test it. We'll log in as your viewer account with curl, call the visits endpoint, and search the raw response for a visitor's name. That's the whole test, it takes fifteen seconds, and it's the most informative thing we do with your submission. Run it on yourself first.

06

The pages

Four pages and a 404 — a small app, not one dashboard. Build it with a router, hash routes, or separate HTML files served by your API. We don't care which, only that the behaviour below holds.

PageWhoWhat's on it
/login
/signup		anyoneSign in, or create an account if you're on the allowed list.
/visitsbothWhere you land after signing in. The list, with filters for date range and visit type, and a few headline numbers above it — including when the lobby is busiest, by day and by hour. That's the question Dana actually has.
/visits/:idbothOne visit on its own page. An admin sees the person; a viewer sees the shape of the visit and no identity.
/admin/usersadmin onlyThe allowed list, and which of those people have actually signed up.
anything else—A real "page not found", not a blank screen.

Five rules for it

  • Every page has its own address. Refresh on any of them — including a single visit — and you're still there and still signed in.
  • Filters live in the URL. Dana filters to interviews in August, pastes the link to a colleague, and it opens already filtered. If that state only exists in memory, the link is useless and so is the feature.
  • Not signed in? You're sent to the login page, and afterwards you land on the page you were trying to reach — not back at the start.
  • Signed in but not allowed? A clean "you don't have access" page. Not a blank screen, not a redirect loop, not a crash. And the navigation doesn't offer a viewer a link that bounces them.
  • Loading and empty states that tell the truth, including about a period where the data can't be relied on. Plain words — no signed_out_at on screen.

Worth being clear about: a route guard is a courtesy for the user, not a security control. Hiding /admin/users from the nav while your API will still answer a viewer's request for it is exactly the gap we're looking for. Both should exist, and you should be able to say which one is doing the real work.

Fine, and encouraged

  • Any framework, or none. A router, hash routing, or plain separate pages.
  • Generating most of the UI with an AI tool.
  • A plain table instead of a chart. Charts are optional here.
  • Serving the pages from your API — one process is fine.

Not needed — skip it

  • AWS or any cloud account. Lambda, S3, Cognito, RDS — none of it. Localhost only.
  • Password reset, 2FA, SSO, OAuth, real emails.
  • Docker, HTTPS, rate limiting, refresh tokens, a production database.
  • A mobile layout, dark mode, animation, visual polish.
